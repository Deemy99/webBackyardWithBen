9 Gardening Mistakes That Make Your Backyard Harder to Maintain

A beautiful backyard shouldn't require every free weekend you have. Many gardens become difficult to maintain simply because of a few decisions made when planting, watering or planning the space.

Gardening often looks simple from the outside: plant something, water it and watch it grow. In reality, some of the most common backyard problems begin long before a plant starts looking unhealthy.

Poor placement, too much water, overcrowding and choosing plants that don't suit the yard can turn a relaxing garden into a constant maintenance project.

The good news is that most of these problems are avoidable. Here are nine common gardening mistakes worth watching for.

01

Planting Before Understanding Your Yard

One of the easiest mistakes to make is buying plants simply because they look good at the garden center.

Before choosing anything, spend a few days watching your backyard. Notice which areas receive direct sun, which stay shaded and where water tends to collect after rain.

A plant that thrives in six hours of sunlight may struggle in a shaded corner, while a shade-loving plant can quickly become stressed in intense afternoon sun.

Ben's Tip

Take a few photos of your yard in the morning, around noon and later in the afternoon. It gives you an easy visual map of where the sun actually goes.

02

Putting Plants Too Close Together

Small plants can make a flower bed look surprisingly empty when they first go into the ground, so it's tempting to plant more of them.

But plants grow.

When shrubs and perennials are packed too tightly, they eventually compete for sunlight, water and nutrients. Dense planting can also reduce airflow, which may make some plant diseases more likely.

Instead of planning around how the plant looks today, check its expected mature width and give it enough room to reach that size.

03

Watering Everything the Same Way

Not every plant in your backyard needs the same amount of water.

A newly planted flower bed may need more frequent attention while established shrubs can often go much longer between watering.

Constant shallow watering can also encourage roots to remain closer to the surface. Depending on the plant and local conditions, deeper watering at appropriate intervals can encourage a stronger root system.

Pay attention to the soil instead of watering purely because the calendar says it's Tuesday.

04

Ignoring Mature Tree and Shrub Size

That small tree sitting in a nursery container may eventually become one of the largest things in your yard.

Planting trees or large shrubs too close to fences, walkways, decks or the house can create headaches years later.

Before planting, look up both the mature height and mature spread. Think about what will be above and below the plant as it grows, not just where the trunk sits today.

05

Leaving Bare Soil Everywhere

Exposed soil between plants may seem harmless, but it can mean more weeds and faster moisture loss.

A reasonable layer of mulch can help suppress weeds, reduce moisture loss and give planting beds a cleaner, finished appearance.

Just avoid piling mulch directly against tree trunks or plant stems. More mulch isn't always better.

06

Choosing High-Maintenance Plants for Every Bed

There's nothing wrong with growing a plant that requires extra attention if you really enjoy it.

The problem comes when nearly everything in the yard needs constant pruning, watering, dividing, deadheading or protection.

A more manageable garden usually mixes a few attention-demanding favorites with dependable plants suited to the local conditions.

Your backyard should leave you time to enjoy it.

07

Forgetting About Drainage

Water has to go somewhere.

If part of your backyard stays soggy long after the rest has dried, repeatedly planting the same types of plants there may not solve the underlying issue.

Observe your yard after heavy rain. Low areas, compacted soil and runoff patterns can all affect how successfully a garden performs.

In some cases, the smartest landscaping decision is to work with the wet conditions rather than constantly fighting them.

08

Buying Plants Without Thinking About Your Climate

A beautiful plant on social media isn't automatically a good plant for your backyard.

Temperature, winter cold, summer heat, rainfall and local soil conditions all matter.

When possible, choose plants suited to your USDA Plant Hardiness Zone and pay attention to local growing recommendations.

Plants that naturally fit your conditions generally require less effort than plants you're constantly trying to keep comfortable.

09

Designing a Garden You Don't Have Time to Maintain

This might be the most important one.

It's easy to design the backyard you'd love to have. It's more useful to design the backyard you'll realistically maintain.

If you enjoy spending Saturday mornings gardening, larger flower beds may be perfect. If you'd rather spend that time grilling with friends or sitting on the patio, simpler planting may make much more sense.

A successful backyard isn't the one with the most plants. It's the one that works for the people who live there.

A Better Garden Can Actually Mean Less Work

Good gardening isn't always about adding more. Sometimes the biggest improvement comes from placing plants where they naturally perform well, giving them enough room and simplifying areas that demand too much attention.

Pay attention to how your backyard behaves throughout the season and make changes gradually. Over time, the goal is a garden that looks good without requiring you to constantly fight against it.
